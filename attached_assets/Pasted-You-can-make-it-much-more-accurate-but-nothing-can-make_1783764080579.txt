You can make it much more accurate, but nothing can make it close to 100% across all tennis matches. Tennis includes injuries, illness, nerves, bad calls, sudden weather changes, unexpected tactical adjustments, and normal randomness. Even accurately predicting a player’s serve direction remains difficult because elite players intentionally behave unpredictably.  

The goal should be:

Become extremely accurate at identifying when the app has strong information—and correctly admitting when a match is genuinely uncertain.

A predictor that says “55%” on close matches and “78%” only on truly favorable matchups is better than one that says “75%” on everything.

The information you need, ranked by importance

1. Point-by-point serve and return data

This is probably the biggest upgrade beyond ordinary rankings and win-loss records.

For each player, collect:

* First-serve points won
* Second-serve points won
* First-serve return points won
* Second-serve return points won
* Service points won
* Return points won
* Service games held
* Return games won
* Break points saved
* Break points converted
* Ace rate per service point
* Double-fault rate per service point
* Performance at 30–30, deuce and break point
* Tiebreak performance
* Results separated by surface

Do not rely mainly on aces or total wins. The strongest base model should estimate:

1. Player A’s probability of winning a service point against Player B.
2. Player B’s probability of winning a service point against Player A.
3. The probability of each player holding serve.
4. The probability of winning each set.
5. The probability of winning the match.

Modern point-based tennis models build match probabilities from estimated serve and return strength, and research continues to show that serve-specific information complements general ratings such as Elo.  

2. Opponent-adjusted statistics

Raw statistics can be misleading.

A player winning 75% of first-serve points against weak ITF opponents is not necessarily serving better than someone winning 71% against top ATP players.

Every statistic should be adjusted for:

* Opponent strength
* Opponent return strength
* Opponent serve strength
* Tournament level
* Surface
* Match format
* Recency
* Quality of competition

Create values such as:

* Adjusted serve strength
* Adjusted return strength
* Adjusted break-point performance
* Adjusted recent form
* Strength of schedule
* Quality-adjusted wins
* Quality-adjusted losses

A loss to the world No. 2 should not hurt a player’s rating like a loss to the world No. 250.

3. Surface-specific ability

Treat players almost like different versions of themselves:

* Player A on clay
* Player A on grass
* Player A on outdoor hard
* Player A on indoor hard

Store separate ratings for:

* Overall Elo
* Clay Elo
* Grass Elo
* Hard-court Elo
* Indoor Elo
* Fast-court Elo
* Slow-court Elo

Surface-specific, time-varying ratings have been used to improve player representations beyond ordinary rankings.  

Also include:

* Career record on the surface
* Last 52 weeks on the surface
* Last 10 matches on the surface
* Serve and return performance on the surface
* Tournament-specific history
* Performance at similar court speeds

Do not treat every hard court as identical.

4. Court-speed and environmental information

The app should understand the conditions, not merely label the match “hard court.”

Collect:

* Indoor or outdoor
* Court pace rating
* Surface manufacturer
* Ball type
* Altitude
* Temperature
* Humidity
* Wind speed
* Wind direction
* Rain or roof status
* Time of day
* Sun and shadow conditions

Why it matters:

* Fast conditions favor many powerful servers.
* Slow conditions create longer rallies.
* Wind can reduce serve effectiveness.
* Altitude can increase ball speed.
* Heat and humidity can affect endurance.
* Indoor conditions remove wind and weather variability.

Use weather at the actual expected match time, not the daily forecast.

5. Real injury and physical-condition information

This may be one of the most valuable—and hardest—categories.

Collect before the prediction cutoff:

* Official withdrawal reports
* Medical timeouts in recent matches
* Recent retirements
* Tape or visible physical restrictions
* Reduced serve velocity
* Reduced movement speed
* Shortened practice sessions
* Missed practices
* Player and coach interviews
* Recent illness reports
* Treatment between matches
* Known chronic injuries
* Sudden statistical decline

Create an injury-risk score, but never treat rumors as confirmed facts.

Use reliability levels:

* Confirmed official information
* Credible reporting
* Player statement
* Visual/statistical warning
* Unverified report

Injury information must be timestamped. Information discovered after the match cannot be placed into the historical prediction.

6. Fatigue and recovery

“Last five matches” is not enough.

Calculate:

* Minutes played during the last 24 hours
* Minutes played during the last 3, 7 and 14 days
* Sets played
* Games played
* Tiebreaks played
* Number of three-set or five-set matches
* Time since previous match
* Previous match finish time
* Travel distance
* Time-zone changes
* Consecutive tournament weeks
* Singles and doubles workload
* Length of recent rallies when available
* Recovery time before the next scheduled match

A player who won three long matches may be in worse physical condition than someone who lost early the previous week.

Build a physical readiness score, not merely a rest-days field.

7. Matchup-specific style data

Tennis is not always transitive:

* Player A may usually beat Player B.
* Player B may usually beat Player C.
* Player C may create serious problems for Player A.

Recent research is specifically exploring intransitive and matchup-dependent tennis strength rather than assuming every player can be placed in one perfect ranking order.  

Collect:

* Left-handed versus right-handed performance
* One-handed versus two-handed backhand
* Big server versus strong returner
* Aggressive baseliner versus defensive counterpuncher
* Net-rushing frequency
* Rally tolerance
* Forehand-to-backhand matchup
* Backhand-to-backhand matchup
* Return position
* Serve direction tendencies
* Performance against similar playing styles
* Performance against similar height and serve profiles

Do not overvalue head-to-head records without context. Old meetings on different surfaces or before major player development may have little relevance.

8. Ball- and shot-tracking data

This is the expensive, advanced layer.

Collect when available:

* Serve speed
* Serve placement
* Serve spin
* First-serve direction by court side and score
* Return depth
* Return location
* Forehand speed
* Backhand speed
* Shot spin
* Shot depth
* Rally length
* Court position
* Distance traveled
* Movement speed
* Change-of-direction speed
* Unforced error patterns
* Forced error patterns
* Winners by shot type
* Net-approach success

ATP’s advanced metrics now include information such as serve, return, forehand and backhand quality based on factors including speed, spin, depth and width.  

Research using point tracking has also identified distinct return-location styles that can improve predictive representation.  

This level can help the model recognize that a player’s results look normal while their actual shot quality or movement is deteriorating.

9. Current-form change detection

Do not use only a fixed last-five or last-ten record.

The app should detect whether the player is:

* Improving
* Declining
* Stable
* Returning from injury
* Adjusting to a new surface
* Playing above an unsustainable level
* Playing better than their results indicate
* Winning despite declining underlying statistics

Track changes in:

* Serve speed
* First-serve percentage
* Second-serve effectiveness
* Return performance
* Break-point creation
* Rally success
* Movement
* Error rate
* Opponent quality

Use multiple time windows:

* Last 3 matches
* Last 5
* Last 10
* Last 30 days
* Last 90 days
* Last 52 weeks

The model should distinguish a real improvement from one unusually good match.

10. Tournament and scheduling context

Include:

* Tournament level
* Round
* Best-of-three or best-of-five
* Qualifying or main draw
* Previous qualifying matches played
* Home-country advantage
* Defending points
* First match after a long break
* First match on a new surface
* Day versus night session
* Expected crowd environment
* Possible schedule disruption

Be careful with “motivation.” It is hard to measure and can become storytelling. Use only measurable evidence.

11. Live warm-up and last-minute information

To produce the best final prediction, run several snapshots:

* 24 hours before
* 6 hours before
* 1 hour before
* 30 minutes before
* 10–15 minutes before

The last snapshot should check:

* Confirmed player participation
* Schedule changes
* Roof status
* Current weather
* Last-minute injury reports
* Warm-up appearance where reliably available
* Player substitutions or withdrawals
* Updated court assignment

Store every version. Do not overwrite the original prediction.

12. Separate models for different competitions

Do not make one universal model control everything.

Use specialist models for:

* ATP
* WTA
* Challenger
* ITF
* Grand Slams
* Best-of-five matches
* Clay
* Grass
* Outdoor hard
* Indoor hard

Data quality and competitive patterns differ across levels. ITF and lower-level matches can have less complete data, so the app should reduce confidence when important information is missing. The ITF provides official player, ranking and event information that can help maintain accurate identities and records across lower-level tours.  

13. A true point-by-point match simulator

After estimating both players’ serve and return strengths, simulate the match at least 50,000–100,000 times.

Each simulation should account for:

* Who serves first
* Surface
* Match format
* Player-specific serve-point probability
* Player-specific return-point probability
* Tiebreak rules
* Fatigue
* Weather
* Injury-risk adjustment
* Probability uncertainty

Output:

* Match winner probability
* Straight-sets probability
* Exact set-score probabilities
* Expected games
* Expected sets
* Tiebreak probability
* Upset probability
* Probability range, not just one number

Do not treat the estimated inputs as perfectly known. Run simulations across plausible input ranges.

Example:

* Central probability: 67%
* Reasonable uncertainty interval: 61%–72%

That is more honest and more useful than pretending the answer is exactly 67%.

14. Market odds as a separate “wisdom of the crowd” model

For maximum predictive accuracy, market prices can be informative because they combine public information, professional models and participant behavior.

But keep two separate outputs:

Independent model

Uses player and match information without sportsbook or prediction-market odds.

Market-enhanced model

Adds:

* Opening odds
* Current odds
* Line movement
* Consensus prices
* Exchange prices
* Market liquidity

Do not simply copy the market. Compare your model with it.

Research has found that combinations of relatively simple models could closely resemble the probability distribution implied by average betting prices, suggesting that the market and predictive models often use overlapping information.  

For your app, show:

* Independent AI probability
* Market-implied probability
* Market-enhanced probability
* Difference between them
* Data confidence
* Final recommended probability

15. Detect when the model should not trust itself

This is one of the most important features.

Lower confidence when:

* Player data is incomplete
* Injury information is uncertain
* Player has few matches on the surface
* Player recently returned from a long absence
* Models strongly disagree
* Estimated probability changes dramatically between updates
* Lower-level match data is unreliable
* Players are closely matched
* The matchup is historically unusual
* The market strongly disagrees and there is no identifiable reason

The app should still give a predicted winner, but label the match:

* Strong information
* Moderate information
* Weak information
* High uncertainty

Astronomical accuracy does not come from forcing more “locks.” It comes partly from identifying which predictions deserve confidence.

The most powerful realistic system

Your final prediction engine should combine:

1. Surface-specific Elo model
2. Serve-and-return point model
3. Opponent-adjusted form model
4. Fatigue and recovery model
5. Injury and availability model
6. Matchup-style model
7. Court and weather model
8. Shot-tracking model
9. Market-independent machine-learning model
10. Market-enhanced model
11. Point-by-point Monte Carlo simulator
12. Confidence-calibration model
13. Data-quality and uncertainty model

Each model should vote, but its weight should depend on how accurately it performed on previous unseen matches of the same type.

What will not make it astronomically accurate

These things can make the application sound advanced without meaningfully improving it:

* Adding thousands of nearly identical models
* Calling an ordinary model “quantum”
* Using more features without verifying their timestamps
* Training on future results
* Repeatedly tuning against the same test set
* Automatically increasing confidence
* Assuming more simulations fix inaccurate inputs
* Treating head-to-head records as decisive
* Using AI-written injury rumors
* Claiming 80–90% accuracy from a small sample

One hundred thousand simulations using bad player probabilities still produce a bad prediction.

Your realistic highest-level objective

Do not program the system to “reach 100%.”

Program it to:

* Obtain the highest-quality pre-match data possible.
* Estimate the matchup at the point level.
* Measure uncertainty honestly.
* Calibrate probabilities on unseen matches.
* Specialize by tour and surface.
* Continuously compare the model with real future outcomes.
* Reserve high confidence for the small group of matches where many independent signals agree.

A very strong result would look like:

Player A projected to win: 72%
Uncertainty range: 66%–76%
Model agreement: 10 of 12 models
Data quality: 94/100
Primary reason: major surface-adjusted serve/return advantage
Main danger: recent physical concern
Prediction status: high confidence, not guaranteed

That is the closest scientifically defensible approach to being “astronomically correct.”
